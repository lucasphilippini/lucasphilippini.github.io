The Britishification of Italy? Explaining the Stability of Meloni’s Government

How Giorgia Meloni turned a country long associated with political instability into one of Europe’s most durable governments — and whether that stability can last.

By Lucas Philippini · October 2026

361 days. That is the average lifespan of an Italian government.

Since the establishment of the Republic in 1946, Italy has had no fewer than 31 prime ministers at the helm of 68 different governments. The figure is a record among Europe’s major parliamentary democracies: Germany has had 25 governments, and Britain only 19 over the same period.

The constant turnover of prime ministers — or, formally, presidents of the Council of Ministers — has become a source of international mockery and domestic frustration in recent decades.

On 4 September, Giorgia Meloni achieved the unexpected. After 1,412 uninterrupted days in office, her government became the longest-serving in post-war Italian history, surpassing that of her political mentor, Silvio Berlusconi.

In the meantime, Germany found itself plunged into an unprecedented political crisis that brought down one government — and threatens a second. The once-stable United Kingdom changed prime ministers three times.

How did Italy, once synonymous with ungovernability, become a model of stability?

La Prima Donna

Giorgia Meloni took office as Italy’s prime minister in October 2022 at the head of a heterogeneous coalition of right-wing parties. Her party, Fratelli d’Italia, emerged from the margins of Italian politics to become the leading force in that year’s parliamentary election.

Winning 26% of the vote on its own, it outperformed Matteo Salvini’s Lega and Berlusconi’s Forza Italia, giving Meloni the mandate to become the first woman to lead the country.

The controversial past of both the prime minister and Fratelli d’Italia cast doubts over the new government — particularly over how it would position itself on the international stage.

In her youth, Meloni was a member of the Italian Social Movement (MSI), a far-right party founded by supporters of fascist dictator Benito Mussolini. Meloni herself once called Mussolini “a good politician” and praised his actions, saying that “everything he did, he did for Italy.”

When the movement was reorganised as Alleanza Nazionale, Meloni became president of its youth wing and, in the early 2000s, was elected to parliament under its banner.

At 31, she became the youngest minister in Italian history when her party joined Berlusconi’s governing coalition. In 2012, she founded Fratelli d’Italia (Brothers of Italy — a reference to the opening line of the Italian national anthem), a fiscally populist, national-conservative and Eurosceptic party.

The party performed poorly in its first elections and chose to remain outside government, lingering on the fringes of Italian politics. In the meantime, Meloni reshaped the party, seeking to distance it from its post-fascist roots and present it as a responsible alternative capable of governing the country.

Meloni — “a woman, a mother, a Christian”, as she emphatically describes herself — nevertheless maintained an inflammatory conservative and sovereigntist rhetoric, coupled with a critical stance towards the European Union, throughout the campaign. Buoyed by the cost-of-living crisis, dissatisfaction with political institutions and opposition to immigration, she took office under Brussels’ wary gaze, seemingly condemned by history to lead yet another short-lived and turbulent Italian government.

Parole, Parole, Parole

Once in office, Giorgia Meloni recalibrated her rhetoric and governed through compromise and moderation, both domestically and internationally.

She bet on a more responsible and institutional foreign policy that minimised friction abroad and strengthened her position as a reliable interlocutor.

With Italy the recipient of more than €200 billion in EU recovery funds, the prime minister cultivated good institutional relations with Brussels — including a close personal relationship with European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen.

As the leader of the bloc’s third-largest economy and commanding considerable political capital, Meloni sought to shape the continent’s agenda and place Italy at the centre of European debates, while proving a reliable ally on key issues.

She became one of the most vocal voices in the debate over fiscal rules and joined other sections of the European right in calling for greater flexibility in the implementation of the European Green Deal.

On migration, she found an unlikely ally in Denmark’s Social Democratic Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen, as the two pushed for a tougher European approach to migration and asylum, including stricter rules on asylum claims and the return of rejected applicants.

On Ukraine, Meloni raised eyebrows as she sidelined the pro-Russian elements within her own coalition — most notably Salvini and Berlusconi — and compared Ukraine’s struggle to the Risorgimento, the 19th-century movement that led to Italian unification.

Her support has been unwavering and has gone beyond words: Italy has been one of Kyiv’s steadfast backers, providing substantial military aid and equipment and backing sanctions against Russia. Meloni was also instrumental in persuading Hungary’s Viktor Orbán to lift his veto on a major EU aid package for Ukraine.

This alignment placed Italy firmly within the NATO and European Union consensus on Russia, defying fears that Meloni would end up siding with Moscow-friendly figures elsewhere in Europe.

Meloni also used her close relationship with Donald Trump to try to bridge the ever-widening gap between Washington and Europe — until relations between the two leaders soured last year.

Domestically, however, the results have been more mixed.

Meloni’s government has largely followed in the footsteps of former Prime Minister Mario Draghi, with her finance minister, Giancarlo Giorgetti, pursuing a tighter fiscal policy that has significantly reduced the budget deficit and lowered borrowing costs. Billions in post-COVID European recovery funds have also helped sustain the economy.

On migration, her rhetoric has collided with the realities of law and politics. Meloni set up migrant processing centres in Albania to handle asylum claims outside Italy. The facilities, expected to house thousands, have received only a few dozen people after becoming entangled in an intense judicial battle over the legality of the scheme. Meanwhile, her government has authorised nearly half a million visas for non-EU workers.

She has, however, cultivated closer relations with several African governments and launched an investment plan for the continent aimed at addressing some of the underlying economic conditions that drive migration.

Nor have the other drastic upheavals once feared by her critics materialised. Meloni’s push for judicial reform, which sought to separate the career paths of judges and prosecutors and create a new disciplinary court, was rejected by 54% to 46% in a referendum earlier this year, dealing her a significant political defeat. Yet her electoral reform, designed to favour the coalition that wins the most votes and make it easier for future governments to secure parliamentary majorities, was approved by the Chamber of Deputies earlier this week.

Cosa Sei?

So what explains the longevity of the current government?

Besides reaping the political dividends of moderating her rhetoric and avoiding many of the costly battles she was once expected to pick — both at home and in Europe — Meloni benefits from a fragmented opposition and the absence of serious challengers within her own coalition.

Italy’s two main opposition parties, Elly Schlein’s Partito Democratico (Democratic Party) and Giuseppe Conte’s Movimento Cinque Stelle (Five Star Movement), remain divided over a range of issues, from who should lead a progressive alliance into the next parliamentary election to the stance it should adopt on Ukraine.

Meanwhile, Meloni’s Brothers of Italy dominates the governing coalition. The party currently polls at around 27%, while Forza Italia stands at roughly 7% and Matteo Salvini’s Lega at little more than 5%.

The main challenge from the right now comes from retired General Roberto Vannacci’s Futuro Nazionale (National Future), which has climbed to around 8% in recent polls. Vannacci presents himself as an alternative to Meloni’s right: a hard-line Eurosceptic who accuses the governing coalition of being too soft on migration and calls for an end to military aid to Ukraine.

Money flowing from Brussels has also helped keep the Italian economy moving, despite frustrating macroeconomic figures.

Meloni’s straightforward manner is seen as an asset by many Italians. It has helped her communicate effectively with her electorate and cultivate an image of reliability and competence among the wider public. She remains one of the country’s most popular — or perhaps least unpopular — political figures, often polling just behind President Sergio Mattarella.

Yet her ability to turn stability and longevity into a lasting legacy remains uncertain — as does the durability of the political formula that has sustained her government. It may therefore still be too early to speak of a “Britishification” of Italian politics.

Italy continues to grapple with stagnant economic growth — GDP expanded by a meagre 0.5% in 2025 — and chronically weak wage growth. Public debt is approaching 139% of GDP, putting the country on course to surpass Greece as the EU’s most indebted member state. Meanwhile, meaningful structural economic reforms remain elusive.

Meloni has also struggled to address Italy’s deepening demographic crisis, with the fertility rate falling to a record low of 1.14 children per woman in 2025. Her flagship constitutional reform of the judiciary was rejected in a referendum earlier this year, depriving her of what could have been a defining political victory.

The longevity of Meloni’s government — sustained by her more moderate approach, a fragmented opposition, the dominance of her party on the right and the continued flow of European funds — is remarkable. Whether this formula can endure is another question altogether.
